Reviewer #1, Comment 3 (did the authors search for existing SIMD implementations?):
  Yes. We looked for existing SIMD implementations of the Mersenne Twister, both
  in the literature and among widely used libraries. What we found falls into
  two groups, and the revised paper now says so explicitly in the new Section 1.1
  (Related work).

  (a) Implementations that vectorize within a single state (intra-state).
  - SFMT (Saito and Matsumoto): a distinct generator designed around 128-bit
    registers; it does not extend to wider registers.
  - Intel MKL: provides MT19937 and SFMT19937 generators. MKL is closed source,
    but its generators reproduce the original sequences, so they necessarily
    operate on a single state. MKL was already one of our baselines (Tables 5
    and 6) and is our strongest competitor: it is faster than V-MT19937 on
    AVX-512 and slower on SSE4.2 and AVX2, and slower than V-SFMT19937 on all
    platforms where both are available.
  - Compiler auto-vectorization of the C++ standard library implementation
    (the STL rows of Tables 5 and 7).
  - Fog (2015): vectorizes MTGP with Mersenne exponent 11213, a different
    member of the family, again within a single state.
  - Our own X-family (X-MT19937, X-MT19937-64, X-SFMT19937), written for this
    paper to have an optimized intra-state implementation of exactly the same
    generators, on all five instruction sets, to compare against.

  (b) Implementations that vectorize across states (inter-state). We found no
  published implementation of MT19937, MT19937-64 or SFMT19937 of this kind.
  The closest work is the multiply-with-carry component of Fog's RANVEC1, which
  runs eight generators with different multipliers in the lanes of one register;
  it is discussed in detail in Section 1.1 and in our response to Reviewer #3,
  Comment 1.

  We cannot exclude that unpublished or proprietary implementations exist, and
  the text is worded accordingly ("we are not aware of").
  Changes: main.tex, Section 1.1 (new paragraph on existing SIMD implementations
  of the Mersenne Twister).

Reviewer #2, Comment 1 (period, sub-sequence treatment, and IID claim):
  We thank the reviewer for this observation. The text has been revised to address
  the period and sub-sequence treatment explicitly.

  Regarding the definition of the sub-streams: the original text defined J as
  P/M, which is not an integer. The construction is now stated directly in terms
  of the M generator instances. With J = (P+1)/M = 2^{19937-log2(M)}, an integer
  since P+1 = 2^19937, instance G_t is advanced by t*J steps with respect to G_0,
  and S is obtained by polling the instances in round-robin order (new Eq. 15
  and revised Eq. 16).

  Regarding the period and the wrap-around: the revised text replaces the
  assertion that S is a permutation of Z with a proof. Position j of S belongs to
  round i = floor(j/M) and instance t = j mod M, so that s_j = z_{(t*J+i) mod P}.
  Since M*J = P+1, we have j*J = i*(P+1) + t*J = i + t*J (mod P), hence
      s_j = z_{(j*J) mod P}                                   (new Eq. 17)
  i.e. S is the decimation of Z with stride J. Since J is a power of two and P is
  odd, J and P are coprime, so the map j -> (j*J) mod P is a bijection of
  {0,...,P-1}. Two consequences follow: (a) the first P elements of S are a
  permutation of one period of Z, so the M instances never overlap within a
  period; (b) s_{j+P} = s_j, so S has period P. No special treatment is needed
  for the fact that P is not a multiple of M: the instances simply wrap around
  the period. The example with P=7, M=2, J=4 is retained as an illustration
  (indices 0,4,1,5,2,6,3).

  The argument assumes that P is the exact period of Z, which holds for MT19937
  and MT19937-64. For SFMT19937 the period is only guaranteed to be a multiple of
  2^19937-1; the text now says so, and for this generator claims only that the
  instances do not overlap over the first P elements of S.

  Regarding the IID claim: the revised text no longer describes the outputs as
  independent and identically distributed, and distinguishes what is proven from
  what is not: (1) period and non-overlap are proven analytically, as above;
  (2) the marginal distribution is unchanged, since S is a reindexing of Z;
  (3) the outputs of each instance, taken alone, are a contiguous segment of the
  original sequence and therefore retain its proven properties; (4) for tuples
  that combine outputs of different instances, as consecutive elements of S do,
  we have no proof, and the text says so. This last point is assessed
  empirically with the TestU01 suite (Section 4.1).

  We wish to be transparent on one further point. The original MT19937 paper
  (Matsumoto & Nishimura, 1998) establishes k-dimensional equidistribution of the
  output sequence as a rigorous mathematical property of the specific recurrence
  and tempering parameters chosen. We were unable to extend this argument to the
  interleaved sequence S: the reordering changes the positions at which consecutive
  k-tuples are drawn from the state, and we could not verify that the
  equidistribution proof carries over. We therefore make no claim that the V-family
  inherits the k-dimensional equidistribution of the original MT19937. The
  statistical validation of the V-family rests entirely on empirical evidence from
  the TestU01 suite, which is the same standard applied to most modern PRNGs.
  Changes: main.tex, Section 3 (lines 304-339).


Reviewer #2, Comment 3 (novelty relative to prior work):
  We agree that the novelty statement was insufficiently precise. The Introduction
  has been restructured and a new Section 1.1 (Related work) has been added to
  define the novelty boundary explicitly.

  The revised text acknowledges that the two ingredients are both established.
  Splitting a sequence into sub-streams by jump-ahead is customarily used to feed
  different threads, processes or machines: each instance runs on its own, and
  each CPU remains poorly utilized since a single instance cannot fill its SIMD
  registers. Running several generators in lock-step in the lanes of one SIMD
  register has also been done before, by Fog (2015), using multiply-with-carry
  generators with a different multiplier in each lane. The contribution of this
  work is to combine the two: the M lanes of a SIMD register are occupied by M
  instances of the same large-state generator, de-phased by jump-ahead so that
  they traverse disjoint segments of a single sequence. One instruction then
  advances all M instances, and the output is an exact rearrangement of the
  sequence of the base generator, for any register width.

  As the reviewer suggests, the contributions are now organised around the state
  layout for inter-state SIMD execution, the uniform treatment of MT19937,
  MT19937-64 and SFMT19937, and the systematic comparison with intra-state
  vectorization (the X-family). Stream splitting across cores and the V-family
  are complementary: multiple V-family generators can be deployed across cores,
  with each core fully utilizing its vector units. The conditions under which the
  technique applies to other generators are stated in the Conclusion (see the
  response on the generalization claim).

  A detailed comparison with the most closely related work is given in the
  response to Reviewer #3, Comment 1.
  Changes: main.tex, line 96 (Introduction), new Section 1.1 (Related work,
  lines 104-121), and Section 5 (Conclusion, line 774).

Reviewer #3, Comment 5 (why does the approach achieve better SIMD utilization?):
  The paper already addresses this question across Sections 2.6 and 3, but the
  connection was not drawn explicitly in one place. A summary sentence has been
  added at the end of the introductory part of Section 3 (before Section 3.1) to
  connect the two.

  The detailed explanation is as follows. Section 2.6 identifies two structural
  obstacles that prevent intra-state SIMD implementations from achieving full
  hardware utilization:

  (1) Residual scalar remainder: the state update is split into sub-loops whose
  iteration counts are generally not multiples of the number of SIMD lanes L/w.
  For MT19937 the first sub-loop has n-m = 624-397 = 227 iterations, which is not
  a multiple of 4, 8 or 16; with SSE2 (4 lanes) this gives 56 vector iterations
  followed by 3 scalar ones. Scalar fallback code is therefore required for the
  remainder iterations.

  (2) Memory misalignment: consecutive groups of state words required by the
  recurrence (x_k, x_{k+1}, ...) are offset by w bytes = 4 bytes for 32-bit words,
  while SIMD loads require alignment to L/8 bytes. This forces costly byte-shift
  or blend intrinsics to assemble operands, and causes cache-line crossing penalties.

  In the V-family, both obstacles are eliminated by the state layout design:
  - Each SIMD lane is permanently assigned to one of M independent states. The
    recurrence advances all M states simultaneously with one SIMD instruction.
    The total recurrence loop is exactly n iterations of one SIMD instruction each,
    with no remainder regardless of n or L/w.
  - All M interleaved state words for position k are contiguous in memory and
    naturally aligned to L/8 bytes, so every SIMD load is aligned with no
    byte-shifting required.
  The result is 100% SIMD lane utilization for the recurrence step, by construction
  rather than as an optimisation target.

  A further advantage noted in the revised Conclusion is implementation simplicity.
  Intra-state vectorization requires careful per-generator analysis of loop structure,
  remainder handling, and misalignment workarounds. The V-family implementation is
  by contrast largely mechanical: extend the state array by a factor of M and replace
  each scalar word operation with its SIMD equivalent. This substitution is uniform
  across generators and ISA widths and can be fully automated via C++ templates.
  Changes: main.tex, Section 3, line 388 (summary sentence) and Section 5 (Conclusion).

Reviewer #2, Comment 2a (near-linear scaling claim):
  We agree that the cross-platform figures in Tables 5--7 cannot isolate SIMD width
  effects from hardware differences, since each column corresponds to a different
  processor. The text has been revised to state this explicitly: the tables are
  intended to be read column-wise, comparing algorithm families on the same hardware.

  To address the reviewer's concern directly, we conducted a controlled experiment on
  a single machine (Intel Xeon Platinum 8488C, GCC 16, -O3 -std=c++20), compiling
  each V-family generator at 128, 256, and 512 bits using explicit ISA flags rather
  than -march=native. The results are presented in a new Table 9 in Section 4.5.

  The controlled results confirm that throughput increases with M but sub-linearly.
  V-MT19937-64 achieves approximately 1.75x per doubling of M (1.74x at 128->256,
  1.80x at 256->512). V-MT19937 scales well from 128 to 256 bits (1.78x) but only
  1.35x from 256 to 512 bits, because at M=16 the combined state (~40KB) approaches
  the L1 cache capacity of the test machine (48KB), increasing memory traffic.
  V-SFMT19937 shows 1.52x from 256 to 512 bits for the same reason. These results
  are consistent with the cache pressure analysis already present in Section 4.5.
  The paper now qualifies the scaling claim as "consistent but sub-linear due to
  memory bandwidth limitations" and attributes the deviation to cache pressure rather
  than algorithmic inefficiency.

Reviewer #1, Comment 4 (benchmark an actual use case):
  We appreciate the suggestion but respectfully decline to add application-level
  benchmarks to this paper. The performance impact of a PRNG on an application
  depends heavily on the application itself: in a Monte Carlo simulation, for
  example, the fraction of total execution time spent in random number generation
  may range from negligible (if the per-sample computation is expensive) to
  dominant (if samples are cheap to process). A single application benchmark would
  therefore not be representative, and a comprehensive survey of application
  domains is beyond the scope of this work. The contribution of this paper is the
  raw throughput improvement of the generator itself, which is the correct metric
  for evaluating a PRNG independently of any particular use case.
  Changes: none.

Reviewer #3, Comment 3 (multi-core implementation):
  The paper already addresses this point in the Introduction (Section 1). The
  purpose of the V-family is to achieve full hardware utilization on a single
  core: M instances run in lock-step so their combined state fills a SIMD register
  exactly, enabling a single instruction to advance all M instances simultaneously.
  Existing approaches that split a sequence into independent sub-streams across
  cores leave each CPU's vector units underutilized — so in effect the focus of
  this paper is precisely on closing this gap by improving utilization of the
  available vector hardware (SIMD).
  Multi-core parallelism is complementary and orthogonal: multiple V-family
  generators, each initialized with a distinct jump-ahead offset to guarantee
  non-overlapping output streams, can be deployed across cores or machines, where
  each core now fully utilizes its own SIMD hardware, compounding the benefit of
  both vectorization and parallelism. This complementarity is stated explicitly in
  Section 1. A full multi-threaded implementation and benchmark is left as future
  work.
  Changes: none (already covered in Section 1).

Reviewer #3, Comment 1 (building blocks are established; Fog 2015, PRAND, Stpiczynski 2020):
  We are grateful to the reviewer for bringing these works to our attention, and
  in particular the paper by Fog, which we had regrettably missed and which is
  indeed the closest prior work. We have studied the three references carefully.
  They led us to correct one statement of the original manuscript and to state
  the contribution more narrowly and, we believe, more accurately.

  We agree with the reviewer that the individual building blocks are established.
  The original Introduction claimed that all existing multi-stream approaches rely
  on unsynchronized generator instances. In the light of Fog's paper this is not
  correct, and the sentence has been removed. The revised manuscript now states
  explicitly that neither jump-ahead stream separation nor lock-step execution of
  several generators in the lanes of a SIMD register is new, and that the
  contribution lies in their combination on a single large-state generator. A new
  Section 1.1 (Related work) positions the V-family against each of the three works.
  The comparison is summarised below.

  (a) Fog (2015).
  Overlap. Fog treats vector and multicore processors together, as we do. His
  RANVEC1 generator has two components. One of them consists of eight 64-bit
  multiply-with-carry (MWC) generators placed in the eight lanes of a 512-bit
  register, advanced together and read in round-robin fashion. This is the same
  structural arrangement as the V-family: one generator per SIMD lane, in
  lock-step. We now credit Fog for it. Fog also reports, for the vectorized MTGP
  component of RANVEC1, the same intra-state obstacles that we describe in
  Section 2.6 (a state buffer whose size is not a multiple of the vector size,
  wrap-around handling, misaligned memory access), and he raises the cache
  footprint of large states as a concern, which our measurements in Section 4.5
  confirm.

  Differences.
  (i) How the lanes are populated. In RANVEC1 each lane holds a different
  generator, obtained with a different multiplier. This ties the construction to
  a generator family for which at least M validated parameter sets exist. Fog
  himself points out that only nine suitable MWC multipliers are known, so that
  the construction cannot be extended beyond 512 bits, and that searching for new
  parameter sets (dynamic creation) is computationally expensive. In the V-family
  the lanes hold instances of the same generator, with its published parameters,
  separated by jump-ahead. No parameter search or generator-specific design is
  needed, and any power of two M is admissible.
  (ii) What the output is. RANVEC1 is a new combined generator (MWC combined with
  MTGP) whose statistical quality has to be established on its own. The output of
  a V-family generator is a rearrangement of the sequence of the base generator:
  the lanes traverse disjoint segments of one sequence, so non-overlap holds by
  construction and the period is unchanged.
  (iii) Where inter-state vectorization is applied. Fog lists jump-ahead among the
  methods for producing independent streams, but does not use it to populate SIMD
  lanes, and the large-state (Mersenne Twister type) component of RANVEC1 remains
  vectorized within a single state. This is possible only because MTGP-11213 was
  selected for its long shortest feedback path. Fog states that SFMT cannot be
  implemented with vectors wider than 128 bits for this reason. The V-family
  applies inter-state vectorization to the large-state generator itself, places
  no requirement on the feedback path, and V-SFMT19937 runs on 256-bit and 512-bit
  registers with the unmodified SFMT19937 recurrence.

  (b) PRAND library.
  Overlap. PRAND provides jump-ahead for MT19937 (and MRG32k3a) and uses it to
  create streams with equally spaced starting points. This is the same operation
  that we use to initialise the M instances.
  Differences. In PRAND the streams are intended for different threads or
  processes and are consumed independently of each other; to our knowledge the
  library does not address SIMD vectorization. PRAND therefore covers the
  stream-separation ingredient only. It is now cited in the Introduction and in
  Section 1.1. Note that multiple independent streams obtained via jump-ahead can
  be used also with the V generators, as it is a complementary technique.

  (c) Stpiczynski (2020).
  Overlap. This work shares with ours the general idea of exploiting the
  linearity of the recurrence to skip ahead in the sequence: the output is split
  into blocks, the 256 numbers that each block needs in order to start are
  computed directly, and the blocks are then generated concurrently.
  Difference. The parallelism is obtained differently. In Stpiczynski's work it
  comes from cores or GPU threads, and SIMD is applied within each block to
  consecutive elements of a single sequence, which is possible for Marsa-LFIB4
  because no number depends on the 54 numbers that precede it. The V-family
  instead places different instances of the generator in the lanes of a SIMD
  register, and therefore does not require such a gap in the recurrence.
  The two approaches are complementary: a block-parallel scheme across cores can
  be combined with inter-state vectorization within each core.

  What is novel. In summary, we do not claim any of the individual ingredients.
  The contribution of the paper is:
  1. the use of jump-ahead substreams of a single large-state F2-linear generator
     as the lanes of a SIMD register, which yields full lane utilization for any
     register width without modifying the generator or searching for parameters;
  2. its uniform application to MT19937, MT19937-64 and SFMT19937, including the
     extension of SFMT19937 beyond 128-bit registers;
  3. a quantitative comparison, on five instruction sets, against optimized
     intra-state implementations of the same generators (the X-family), including
     the case in which the inter-state approach loses because of cache pressure
     (MT19937 at 512 bits).
  Changes: main.tex, line 96 (Introduction, novelty paragraph rewritten), new
  Section 1.1 (Related work, lines 104-121), Section 5 (Conclusion, line 774), and
  bibliography (three references added: Fog 2015, Stpiczynski 2020, PRAND).

Reviewer #1, Comment 2 / Reviewer #2, Comment 3c (jump-ahead not benchmarked; initialization time claimed "negligible" with no supporting numbers):
  We agree, and we thank the reviewers for insisting on this point: measuring
  the initialization showed that our original statement was too optimistic.
  The sentence stating that the initialization overhead "is negligible" has been
  removed, and the jump-ahead is now benchmarked.

  Section 3.1.2 has been renamed "Polynomial jump-ahead (default method)" and
  expanded. A new equation (Eq. 22) states the polynomial evaluation formula
  explicitly, followed by a cost analysis and by measurements.

  Cost analysis. Evaluating the jump polynomial requires N applications of the
  state-transition operator (N = 19937 for MT19937 and MT19937-64). In addition,
  for every non-zero coefficient, i.e. for about half of them, the current state
  is accumulated into the result with an XOR over all its n words. One jump
  therefore costs N generator steps plus about N*n/2 word operations (about
  6 x 10^6 for MT19937), and M instances require M-1 jumps.

  Measurements. A new Section 4.4 (Initialization cost), with a new Table 8,
  reports the measured cost of the complete
  initialization (including the M-1 jumps), for both the polynomial and the
  matrix method. The cost is expressed as the number of random numbers that the
  same generator produces in vectorial mode in the time taken by its
  initialization (SSE4.2 platform of the paper, 128-bit hardware registers):

                              polynomial      matrix
    V-MT19937,    M=4          77 x 10^6     47 x 10^6
    V-MT19937,    M=8         200 x 10^6    110 x 10^6
    V-MT19937,    M=16        340 x 10^6    150 x 10^6
    V-MT19937-64, M=2         6.2 x 10^6    7.0 x 10^6
    V-MT19937-64, M=4          21 x 10^6     22 x 10^6
    V-MT19937-64, M=8          57 x 10^6     48 x 10^6
    V-SFMT19937,  M=2          14 x 10^6     31 x 10^6
    V-SFMT19937,  M=4          34 x 10^6     68 x 10^6

  The complete initialization therefore costs as much as generating between
  6 x 10^6 and 3.4 x 10^8 random numbers with the polynomial method, and between
  7 x 10^6 and 1.5 x 10^8 with the matrix method (not counting the time to load
  the 50 MB matrix from disk). The paper now states that the initialization cost
  is negligible only for applications drawing a much larger number of samples
  from each generator.

  Regarding the choice of method (Reviewer #2): the measurements show that the
  polynomial method is not faster than the matrix method for MT19937. It is the
  default because of its memory footprint (about 4 KB per jump polynomial
  instead of about 50 MB per jump matrix), and the text now says so. The matrix
  subsection also states its cost explicitly: one vector-matrix multiplication
  of O(N^2/w) bit operations and O(N^2) bits of storage.
  Changes: main.tex, Section 3.1.2 (renamed, expanded, Eq. 22) and new
  Section 4.4 (Initialization cost, Table 8).

Reviewer #3, Comment 4 (implementation details insufficient):
  We agree that the paper described the algorithm but not its concrete realization
  in hardware. A new Section 3.2 "Implementation notes" has been added (before the
  Test Results section) covering four topics:

  Memory layout. The M generator states are stored as a single flat, cache-line-
  aligned array m_state[n×M] of w-bit words. Position i·M+t holds the i-th word
  of the t-th generator, so the M copies of position i across all generators form
  M consecutive, naturally aligned words. Every SIMD load that advances position i
  is therefore aligned; no gather, scatter, or byte-shift is needed.

  Core recurrence loop. Each of the n iterations reads three SIMD-width groups
  (positions k, k+1 mod n, k+m mod n) via aligned loads and writes one back. The
  recurrence (Equation 18) maps to 4--5 SIMD instructions per iteration: a bit-
  select merging the upper bit of x_k with the lower 31 bits of x_{k+1}, a right
  shift by one, and a conditional XOR with matrix constant A. The loop consists of
  exactly n SIMD iterations with no scalar remainder, regardless of n or M.

  Tempering and output. After refill, a separate pass applies tempering in blocks
  of M words using SIMD shifts and masked XOR-AND operations. Tempered output is
  buffered and consumed one element at a time until exhausted.

  Portability. A template class SimdRegister<VRegBitLen, ISA> in SIMD.h is
  specialised for each (width, ISA) pair: x86 SSE4.2, AVX2, AVX-512, and ARM
  NEON, SVE256. A new Table (tab:intrinsics) lists the hardware instructions for
  each abstract operation (load/store, XOR/AND, shift, bitwiseSelect,
  xorIfOddCst32) across all five ISAs. The two non-trivial operations vary most:
  bitwiseSelect requires 3 instructions on SSE4.2/AVX2 but only 1 on AVX-512
  (VPTERNLOGD) and NEON/SVE2 (BSL); xorIfOddCst32 requires 4 instructions on
  SSE4.2 and NEON/SVE, 3 on AVX2, and only 2 on AVX-512 (VPTESTMD + masked
  VPXORD). When the virtual register width exceeds the hardware width, operations
  are emulated automatically by iterating over multiple hardware registers.
